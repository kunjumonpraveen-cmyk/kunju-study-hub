Learning Outcomes

The objective is not to memorise petroleum terminology. It is to understand the chain from loss of containment → dispersion → ignition → fire/explosion → escalation.

1. Hydrocarbon Fundamentals

Hydrocarbons are compounds composed primarily of hydrogen and carbon. In industry they range from gases to volatile liquids and heavy oils.

Crude oil

A complex mixture of hydrocarbons and other compounds requiring separation and processing.

Natural gas

Primarily methane, with varying quantities of other hydrocarbons and impurities.

Petroleum products

Products such as gasoline, diesel, kerosene, LPG and other refinery streams.

Why hydrocarbons are dangerous

The key hazard is not simply that a material “burns.” A credible loss of containment can create a flammable mixture in air. If an ignition source is present, the result can range from a local fire to a major accident involving multiple units.

Loss of containment + Air + Ignition = Potential fire/explosion

2. Properties & Flammability

Flash point

The lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off sufficient vapour under specified test conditions to form an ignitable mixture near its surface. Flash point is a key indicator of fire hazard for liquids.

Auto-ignition temperature

The minimum temperature at which a substance can ignite in air without an external ignition source under specified conditions.

LEL and UEL

TermMeaningSafety significance
LELLower flammable limitBelow this concentration, the mixture is generally too lean to burn.
Flammable rangeBetween LEL and UELA mixture can potentially ignite if an ignition source is present.
UELUpper flammable limitAbove this concentration, the mixture is generally too rich to burn; dilution with air can bring it into the flammable range.

Vapour density

Vapour density compares the density of a vapour to air. Vapours heavier than air can travel along low areas and accumulate in pits, drains or poorly ventilated spaces.

Important: “Heavier than air” does not mean a vapour will always remain at floor level. Wind, turbulence, temperature, release momentum and ventilation strongly affect dispersion.

3. Hydrocarbon Release Scenarios

A release assessment considers the source, pressure, temperature, phase, quantity, duration, location, ventilation and nearby ignition sources.

Small leak

May create a local flammable atmosphere or flash fire if ignited.

Pressurised release

Can produce a high-velocity jet and rapid vapour dispersion.

Large liquid release

Can form a pool and generate significant vapour.

Loss of containment thinking

  1. What failed?
  2. What material was released?
  3. At what pressure and temperature?
  4. Where will the material travel?
  5. Where can it accumulate?
  6. What ignition sources exist?
  7. What equipment could be exposed to heat?
  8. Can isolation stop the release?

4. Hydrocarbon Fire Types

ScenarioDescriptionMajor concern
Pool fireFire over a pool of liquid fuel.High radiant heat and escalation to nearby equipment.
Jet firePressurised flammable release burning as a directed flame.Severe local heat flux and equipment damage.
Flash fireFlammable vapour cloud ignites and burns through the cloud.Rapid flame travel through the cloud.
Vapour cloud explosionA flammable cloud ignites under conditions capable of producing significant overpressure.Blast damage and secondary fires.
BLEVECatastrophic failure of a pressurised vessel containing a liquid above its atmospheric boiling point, often associated with external heating.Fireball, fragments and severe thermal/physical effects.
BLEVE is not simply “any LPG fire.” The mechanism involves vessel failure and rapid depressurisation of a pressurised liquid system. External fire exposure is a classic initiating condition but is not the only consideration.

5. LPG, LNG & CNG

LPG

Liquefied petroleum gas, commonly propane, butane or mixtures. Stored as a pressurised liquid.

LNG

Liquefied natural gas, predominantly methane, maintained at cryogenic temperature.

CNG

Compressed natural gas stored at high pressure, normally remaining gaseous.

Different physical behaviour

MaterialKey hazard characteristicImportant control concept
LPGPressurised liquefied gas; vapour can be flammable and may accumulate in low areas depending on composition and conditions.Leak prevention, isolation, ventilation, ignition control and vessel protection.
LNGCryogenic liquid; rapid vaporisation can create a large gas volume and cold-burn hazards.Containment, exclusion zones, ignition control and cryogenic protection.
CNGHigh-pressure gas with stored energy.Pressure control, isolation, leak detection and mechanical protection.

6. Petroleum Storage & Tank Farms

Tank farms combine large inventories of flammable materials with transfer operations, piping, pumps, ignition sources and potential domino effects.

Major hazards

Tank overfill

Can cause large liquid release and vapour generation.

Seal/fire

Fire can occur around floating-roof tank seals or other release points.

Bund/dike failure

Loss of secondary containment can increase spill consequences.

Lightning

Potential ignition source requiring appropriate protection.

Static

Charge accumulation during transfer can create ignition potential.

Domino effect

Heat from one burning vessel can threaten adjacent equipment.

Fire protection philosophy

Protection may include fixed water systems, foam systems, monitors, hydrants, detection, alarms, emergency shutdown, drainage/containment and organised emergency response. The exact system depends on product, tank type, inventory, layout and applicable standards.

7. Loading & Unloading Operations

Transfer operations are high-risk because hoses, arms, valves, pumps, vehicles and human actions interact.

Transfer checklist

  • Correct product and receiving system identified.
  • Vehicle/tank correctly positioned and secured.
  • Connections inspected and compatible.
  • Isolation valves identified.
  • Overfill protection available and functional where required.
  • Bonding/earthing arrangements followed according to the system.
  • Ignition sources controlled.
  • Emergency shutdown arrangements understood.
  • Spill response equipment available.

8. Pipeline Safety

Hydrocarbon pipelines can fail through corrosion, mechanical damage, overpressure, defective equipment, operational error or external interference.

Key controls

Integrity management

Inspection, corrosion control, monitoring and maintenance.

Isolation

Sectionalising valves can limit release inventory.

Leak detection

Pressure, flow, instrumentation and other detection methods can identify abnormal conditions.

Right-of-way control

Prevent excavation or third-party damage around pipelines.

9. Gas Detection & Emergency Shutdown

Detection and shutdown systems provide early warning and can reduce the duration or scale of a release. They are protective layers, not substitutes for good containment design.

Gas detection

Flammable gas

Detects concentrations approaching hazardous flammability conditions.

Toxic gas

Detects substances such as hydrogen sulfide where relevant.

Oxygen

Monitors oxygen deficiency or enrichment in applicable spaces.

Emergency shutdown

An ESD system is designed to place a process or part of a facility into a safer state during defined abnormal conditions. Typical functions may include closing valves, stopping pumps or isolating equipment.

Safety-system reliability matters. Detection, alarm and shutdown systems need appropriate design, testing, maintenance and management of bypasses or impairments.

10. Foam Firefighting

Foam is widely used for certain flammable-liquid fire scenarios because it can form a blanket over the fuel surface, reduce vapour release and separate the fuel from the flame.

Basic mechanisms

Blanketing

Creates a layer over the fuel surface.

Vapour suppression

Reduces release of flammable vapour from the liquid surface.

Cooling

Water in the foam solution contributes to cooling.

Foam system considerations

System selection depends on the fuel, storage arrangement, fire scenario, application method, required application rate and applicable standard. Foam concentrate compatibility and system proportioning are critical.

Do not assume all foam concentrates are interchangeable. Use the concentrate, proportioning equipment and application system for which the installation is designed.

11. Ignition Sources & Static Electricity

Hydrocarbon fire prevention depends heavily on preventing the release and controlling potential ignition sources.

Electrical

Arcs, sparks, defective equipment or unsuitable electrical installations.

Hot surfaces

Equipment surfaces can exceed auto-ignition temperatures.

Vehicles

Engines, exhausts and electrical systems can become ignition sources.

Hot work

Welding, cutting and grinding introduce direct ignition sources.

Static discharge

Accumulated charge can produce a spark capable of ignition.

Lightning

Natural ignition source requiring engineered protection and procedures.

Static-control principles

Control can involve bonding, earthing/grounding, appropriate transfer procedures, flow control and avoiding conditions that promote charge accumulation. The correct engineering approach depends on the material and system.

12. Hydrocarbon Emergency Response

The first priority in a major hydrocarbon incident is protecting people and preventing escalation. Firefighting decisions must be based on the incident command system, competent emergency responders and the specific scenario.

Initial response sequence

  1. Raise the alarm.
  2. Protect people: evacuate or shelter according to the emergency plan.
  3. Isolate the source if this can be done safely through designed systems.
  4. Control ignition sources where appropriate.
  5. Establish exclusion zones.
  6. Cool exposed equipment where the emergency strategy calls for it.
  7. Extinguish only when the release is controlled or the strategy is otherwise safe.
  8. Monitor for re-ignition and escalation.
Critical principle: Extinguishing a hydrocarbon flame while a significant release continues can create a dangerous unignited vapour cloud. The incident strategy must consider release isolation and re-ignition risk.

Emergency planning

Plans should define alarm arrangements, roles, communications, assembly areas, access for emergency services, isolation points, firefighting resources, medical response, mutual aid and escalation criteria.
