Fire Prevention & Protection Systems

1. The fire-protection strategy

A good facility does not depend on one extinguisher or one sprinkler. Fire safety is a layered defence.

Prevent

Control ignition sources, combustible loading, electrical hazards, hot work and unsafe processes.

Detect

Recognise smoke, heat, flame or other abnormal conditions early.

Alert

Transmit an alarm so people and emergency systems can respond.

Contain

Use walls, floors, doors and fire stopping to limit spread.

Suppress

Use sprinklers, hydrants, foam or other appropriate systems to control fire.

Escape

Provide protected routes, exits, emergency lighting and clear evacuation arrangements.

Prevention → Detection → Alarm → Containment → Suppression → Evacuation → Emergency Response
Engineering principle: A protection system is only as strong as its weakest critical dependency. A sprinkler system with a closed control valve, an alarm with disabled notification appliances or a fire door wedged open can create a major barrier failure.

2. Portable fire extinguishers

2.1 Purpose

Portable extinguishers provide first-aid firefighting for an incipient fire when the extinguisher is suitable, the user is trained and conditions permit safe intervention. They are not a substitute for fixed protection, emergency planning or evacuation.

Agent / typeTypical applicationImportant limitation
WaterSuitable ordinary combustible firesNot universally suitable; electrical and water-reactive hazards require care.
FoamSuitable Class A and selected flammable-liquid applicationsCompatibility and application method matter.
Dry chemicalMany flammable-liquid/gas and some electrical applicationsLimited cooling; re-ignition can occur.
CO₂Selected electrical and flammable-liquid hazardsLimited cooling; ineffective for many deep-seated combustibles.

2.2 Inspection logic

  1. Correct type and rating for the hazard.
  2. Accessible, visible and unobstructed.
  3. Correct mounting/location according to applicable requirements.
  4. Pressure/indicator condition where provided.
  5. Pin, seal, hose/nozzle and body condition.
  6. No obvious corrosion, leakage or damage.
  7. Inspection/maintenance status current.
Do not train students to fight fires beyond their capability. If smoke is heavy, fire is spreading, the escape route is threatened, or the fuel is unknown, evacuation and emergency response take priority.

3. Hydrant, hose reel & water distribution systems

3.1 Basic architecture

Fire water storage ↓ Fire pump(s) ↓ Main fire-water network ↓ Risers / hydrant mains / branch lines ↓ Hydrants / hose reels / other outlets ↓ Hose + nozzle → Firefighting stream

3.2 Key components

Fire water tank

Provides the required water reserve. Level, accessibility and protection against contamination/freezing where relevant matter.

Isolation valves

Allow sections to be isolated. Their normal position is critical to system readiness.

Hydrant

Provides a connection for firefighting hoses in the designed system.

Hose reel

Provides a permanently arranged hose for initial firefighting where the system is designed for it.

3.3 Pressure and flow

Fire-water systems must deliver adequate flow and pressure at the intended outlets. Losses occur through pipe friction, fittings, elevation and other system effects.

Pressure available at outlet ≈ Pump pressure − system losses − elevation effects

Actual design calculations require the applicable code, hydraulic data, pipe characteristics and system configuration. Never treat a simplified formula as a design calculation.

4. Fire pumps

Fire pumps provide the pressure and flow required by a fixed water-based fire-protection system. A typical arrangement may include a main electric pump, diesel pump and jockey pump, depending on the installation and applicable requirements.

PumpGeneral role
Jockey pumpMaintains system pressure and compensates for small losses without starting the main pump for every minor pressure change.
Electric main pumpProvides substantial flow/pressure when the system demand requires it.
Diesel fire pumpProvides an alternative power source where the system design requires it.

4.1 Pump-room inspection mindset

  • Is the room accessible and free of unnecessary storage?
  • Are pumps, controllers and power/fuel arrangements available?
  • Are valves in their required normal positions?
  • Is there evidence of leakage, overheating, corrosion or abnormal condition?
  • Are gauges readable and plausible?
  • Are routine test records available?
  • Is there an impairment or isolation notice?
Important: A pump that runs during a test is not automatically proof that the entire fire-water system is compliant. System performance depends on the complete hydraulic network and required testing regime.

5. Automatic sprinkler systems

5.1 How a sprinkler operates

Most conventional sprinklers are individually heat operated. A sprinkler head normally does not discharge simply because a smoke detector senses smoke. Heat causes the operating element to activate at its designed temperature condition, allowing water to discharge.

Fire develops ↓ Heat accumulates near ceiling ↓ Sprinkler operating element reaches activation condition ↓ Individual sprinkler opens ↓ Water discharges onto fire / hot area ↓ Flow causes alarm / waterflow indication ↓ Additional system response as designed

5.2 Key components

Sprinkler head

Heat-operated discharge device.

Control valve

Controls water supply to a system or zone and must remain in the required position.

Alarm valve / trim

Provides system functions and waterflow/alarm arrangements according to system type.

Waterflow switch

Detects sustained water movement and can initiate alarm signals.

Test & drain

Allows testing and controlled drainage.

Fire pump

Supports water supply where required by system design.

5.3 Common sprinkler systems

SystemConceptTypical consideration
Wet pipePipe network normally contains water.Common where temperatures permit.
Dry pipePipe contains pressurised gas until activation releases water.Useful where freezing is a concern.
Pre-actionAdditional detection/control sequence before water enters piping in many designs.Used where accidental water discharge has significant consequences.
DelugeOpen nozzles; system releases water over an area when activated.Used for high-hazard applications requiring rapid application.
Do not obstruct sprinklers. Storage too close to sprinkler heads, damaged heads, painted heads or changes to ceiling geometry can compromise designed discharge performance.

6. Fire detection & alarm systems

6.1 Detection types

Smoke detectors

Designed to detect smoke particles using technologies such as photoelectric or ionisation principles, depending on device type.

Heat detectors

Respond to temperature reaching a threshold or a rapid rate of temperature rise, depending on type.

Flame detectors

Detect characteristic optical radiation from flames; useful in selected industrial hazards.

Gas detectors

Detect specific gases or vapours; commonly important for flammable or toxic gas hazards.

6.2 Alarm chain

Detector → Fire Alarm Control Panel → Notification / Indication → Emergency Response

Modern systems may also transmit signals to remote monitoring, building management systems, smoke control systems, elevators, access control or other interfaces where designed.

6.3 False alarms

Repeated unwanted alarms should not simply be silenced. Identify environmental causes, detector selection, contamination, cooking vapour, dust, steam, system design and maintenance issues.

7. Passive fire protection

Passive protection does not normally require active operation at the moment of fire. It is built into the building or process environment to resist or limit fire spread.

Compartmentation

Fire-resisting walls/floors divide a building into zones.

Fire doors

Maintain compartment boundaries when correctly installed, closed and maintained.

Fire stopping

Seals penetrations and joints so services do not create hidden fire paths.

Structural protection

Protects structural members against heat exposure for the required performance.

Fire-rated glazing

Provides a controlled opening while maintaining specified fire performance.

Dampers

Can limit fire/smoke spread through ventilation systems where designed and maintained.

Common failure: A fire-rated wall is only as effective as its penetrations. An unprotected cable tray, pipe or poorly sealed opening can undermine the compartment boundary.

8. Emergency lighting, exits & life safety

8.1 Means of escape

Fire protection is not only about extinguishing fire. People need a reliable path from occupied spaces to a place of safety. Egress design involves travel distance, number and width of exits, exit separation, protected stairs, doors, signage and occupancy.

8.2 Emergency lighting

Emergency lighting supports visibility when normal power fails. It should be positioned and maintained so occupants can identify routes, changes in level, exits and essential safety equipment as required by the applicable standard.

8.3 Door and corridor discipline

  • Do not wedge fire doors open unless the system is specifically designed and approved for controlled hold-open operation.
  • Do not store materials in escape routes.
  • Keep exits unlocked and available as required during occupancy.
  • Maintain clear signage and emergency lighting.

9. Inspection, testing & maintenance

A protection system is not “installed and finished.” Readiness is maintained through inspection, testing, maintenance, records and impairment management.

LayerPurposeExample
InspectionLook for condition/readiness issues.Valve position, damage, obstruction.
TestingOperate or measure a function.Alarm test, pump test, flow test.
MaintenanceRestore or preserve performance.Repair valve, replace detector, service pump.
RecordsDemonstrate what was done and identify trends.Test certificates, logs, defect reports.

9.1 Impairment management

If a sprinkler system, fire pump, alarm system or critical fire barrier is unavailable, the situation should be treated as an impairment. The facility needs a defined process for notification, risk assessment, temporary measures, responsible persons and restoration.

Never conceal an impairment. A disabled protection system is a change in risk and should trigger the site's approved impairment procedure.

10. Design and inspection scenarios

Scenario A — Sprinkler valve found closed

This is potentially a critical impairment. Verify which system is affected, prevent unauthorised operation of the valve, notify responsible personnel, follow impairment procedures and restore the system as soon as safely possible.

Scenario B — Fire pump starts repeatedly every few minutes

Do not assume this is normal. Investigate pressure loss, leakage, valve condition, test arrangements and controller settings. Frequent cycling can indicate a system defect.

Scenario C — Fire door permanently wedged open

The door may no longer provide the intended compartmentation. Identify why it is held open. If operational access requires an open door, a compliant hold-open arrangement may be needed rather than an improvised wedge.

Scenario D — Storage stacked close beneath sprinklers

Storage arrangement can interfere with sprinkler discharge and heat collection. Check the applicable design criteria, commodity classification, storage height and required clearances rather than relying on a generic distance.

Essential glossary

Alarm valve

Valve arrangement used in certain water-based systems to control and signal water flow.

Compartmentation

Division of a building into fire-resisting compartments to limit fire and smoke spread.

Fire door

Door assembly designed and tested to provide specified fire resistance.

Fire pump

Pump used to provide required water flow/pressure for fire-protection systems.

Impairment

Condition where a fire-protection system or barrier is unavailable or degraded.

Jockey pump

Small pump used to maintain system pressure and compensate for minor losses.

Sprinkler

Heat-operated device that releases water in a designed pattern when activated.

Waterflow switch

Device that detects water movement and can initiate a signal.

Fire stopping

Measures that maintain fire-resistance around penetrations, joints and service openings.
