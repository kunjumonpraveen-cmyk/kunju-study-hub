Subject 02 • Fire science from first principles

Fire Science & Fire Engineering

Start with the physics and chemistry of fire, then build toward prediction, fire behaviour, extinguishment and engineering judgement. The objective is not to memorise the fire triangle—it is to understand why fires ignite, grow, spread, produce smoke and can be controlled.

1. What is fire?

Fire is a rapid, exothermic oxidation process accompanied by heat and often light. Not every oxidation reaction produces a visible flame; the word “fire” usually refers to combustion occurring at a rate capable of producing significant thermal and/or radiant effects.

Start with this: Fire is not a substance. It is a process—a chemical reaction in which energy is released and can sustain further reaction.

1.1 The fire triangle

The traditional fire triangle contains three elements:

Fuel

Material capable of undergoing combustion: solid, liquid vapour or gas.

Oxidiser

Usually oxygen from air, but some materials and processes can provide or release oxidising species.

Heat

Sufficient energy to raise fuel to ignition conditions and sustain the reaction.

1.2 The fire tetrahedron

The tetrahedron adds a fourth element: the chemical chain reaction. This helps explain why some extinguishing agents are effective even when they do not simply “remove oxygen”.

Fuel + Oxidiser + Heat + Sustaining Chain Reaction → Sustained Combustion

Extinguishment can therefore target one or more sides of this model: remove fuel, remove heat, reduce oxidiser availability, or interrupt the chemical reaction.

1.3 Fire versus oxidation

Oxidation can be slow, such as corrosion, or extremely rapid, such as an explosion. The rate of reaction strongly affects the observable behaviour and energy release.

2. Heat, temperature & energy

2.1 Heat is not temperature

Temperature describes the thermal state of a material. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Confusing these concepts leads to poor fire analysis.

2.2 Heat transfer

ModeHow it worksFire example
ConductionEnergy transfer through a material by molecular interaction.A steel beam heated on one side transfers heat toward another area.
ConvectionHeat transported by moving fluids such as air and hot gases.Hot smoke rises and carries heat through a building.
RadiationElectromagnetic energy transfer; no intervening medium is required.A large fire radiates heat to nearby surfaces and can ignite them.
Fire-engineering habit: When considering fire spread, ask separately: “Can heat travel through the structure?”, “Can hot gases move there?”, and “Can radiant heat reach the target?”

2.3 Heat flux

Radiant and convective exposure can be expressed as heat flux—energy received per unit area per unit time. High heat flux can cause ignition, painful exposure, material degradation or structural damage.

3. Combustion science

Combustion involves chemical reactions between fuel and an oxidising species. For many common fires, the fuel must first produce vapour or gaseous decomposition products before flaming combustion can occur.

3.1 Solid fuels and pyrolysis

Many solids do not burn directly as a solid surface. Heating can cause pyrolysis: thermal decomposition that releases combustible gases and vapours. Those gases mix with oxygen and burn above the surface.

Solid fuel ↓ heating Pyrolysis / decomposition ↓ Combustible vapours + gases ↓ + oxygen + ignition source Flame ↓ Heat feedback to fuel ↺ continued pyrolysis

3.2 Liquid fuels

Most liquid fires are fundamentally vapour fires. The liquid evaporates; the vapour mixes with air; the vapour-air mixture burns above the liquid surface. Temperature, vapour pressure, surface area and ventilation influence burning behaviour.

3.3 Gaseous fuels

A gas already exists in a potentially combustible form. The critical issue becomes concentration in air, mixing, ignition source and confinement. A released gas can form a flammable cloud before ignition.

3.4 Stoichiometry — introductory idea

Combustion can be represented by chemical equations. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon ideally produces carbon dioxide and water, but real fires often produce carbon monoxide, soot and other products because mixing and oxygen availability are imperfect.

4. Ignition, flash point & flammability

4.1 Ignition

Ignition occurs when a fuel reaches conditions where the rate of heat generation and chemical reaction can support sustained combustion. An ignition source may be a flame, hot surface, spark, electrical arc, static discharge or other energy source.

4.2 Flash point

The flash point of a liquid is the lowest temperature at which it gives off enough vapour to form an ignitable mixture with air under specified test conditions. A flash point does not mean the liquid will necessarily sustain burning after the ignition source is removed.

4.3 Fire point

The fire point is a higher temperature at which vapour generation is sufficient to sustain combustion under the specified test conditions.

4.4 Auto-ignition temperature

The auto-ignition temperature is the approximate minimum temperature at which a substance can ignite in air without an external ignition source under specified conditions. It is not the same as flash point.

4.5 Flammable limits

A gas or vapour generally burns only when its concentration in air lies within a particular range:

LEL / LFLLower concentration boundary of the flammable range.
UEL / UFLUpper concentration boundary of the flammable range.
Critical distinction: A mixture below the LEL is too lean to burn; a mixture above the UEL may be too rich to burn at that moment. A rich mixture can become dangerous as it mixes with air and moves into the flammable range.

4.6 Fire triangle versus flammability

Having fuel, oxygen and an ignition source does not guarantee burning. The fuel-air mixture must also be within the appropriate concentration and temperature conditions for the relevant combustion process.

5. Fire development

Fire behaviour depends on fuel arrangement, ventilation, geometry, heat feedback and suppression. A simple fire-growth model is useful, but real fires can deviate substantially.

5.1 Typical stages

Incipient

Ignition begins; energy release may initially be small.

Growth

Flame and heat release increase; nearby materials may ignite.

Fully developed

Large-scale burning occurs and heat release can become extreme.

Decay

Fuel or oxygen becomes limiting and heat release decreases, although dangerous hot gases and smouldering can remain.

Flashover

A rapid transition to widespread involvement of combustible surfaces in a compartment as thermal conditions become severe.

5.2 Heat release rate

Heat release rate (HRR) is one of the most important quantities in fire engineering. It describes how quickly a fire releases energy, commonly expressed in kW or MW.

HRR is a measure of fire size in terms of energy released per unit time.

A larger HRR generally means greater heat production, stronger convective flows, more smoke production and potentially faster fire growth.

5.3 Ventilation-controlled fires

In an enclosure, oxygen availability can limit burning. Opening a door or window can change the ventilation regime and may cause a dangerous increase in burning rate.

6. Smoke & toxic fire products

For many occupants, smoke is the dominant immediate life-safety hazard in a building fire.

6.1 What smoke contains

Smoke is a complex mixture of hot gases, vapours, aerosols and particles. Composition varies with fuel, temperature and ventilation.

Carbon monoxide (CO)

A toxic product of incomplete combustion; it can impair oxygen transport and cellular function.

Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

Produced during combustion and can contribute to hypercapnia and oxygen displacement at high concentrations.

Hydrogen cyanide (HCN)

Can be produced from nitrogen-containing materials and is highly toxic.

Smoke particulates

Can irritate the respiratory system, obscure vision and transport toxic substances.

6.2 Smoke movement

Hot smoke tends to rise because of buoyancy. It can form a hot upper layer beneath a ceiling and move through corridors, shafts, stairwells and other pathways. Pressure differences, wind and mechanical ventilation can strongly alter movement.

Evacuation lesson: Never assume that a corridor or stairwell is safe simply because flames are not visible. Smoke can precede the flame front and can make an escape route untenable.

7. Classification of fires

Fire classes are defined differently across standards and jurisdictions. Always use the classification system applicable to the equipment and authority having jurisdiction.

Class BFlammable liquidsPetrol, solvents, paintsSpreading and vapour ignition are major concerns.
Class C / gas classesFlammable gases in systems using such classificationLPG, propane, methaneControl of fuel supply is critical.

Do not rely on class letters alone. Fire-class terminology differs between standards. Read the extinguisher rating, follow local requirements and select an agent compatible with the actual hazard.

8. Fire behaviour

8.1 Flashover

Flashover is a rapid transition during which exposed combustible surfaces in a compartment become involved as the upper gas layer and radiant environment reach severe conditions. It is a major survivability threshold.

8.2 Backdraft

Backdraft can occur when a ventilation-limited compartment contains hot, fuel-rich gases and oxygen is suddenly introduced. Rapid combustion can follow. The phenomenon is different from flashover, although both can be extremely dangerous.

8.3 Flame types

Diffusion flame

Fuel and oxidiser mix at or near the reaction zone. Many common fires behave this way.

Premixed flame

Fuel and oxidiser are mixed before ignition within appropriate concentration limits.

Smouldering

Flaming may be absent; slow surface or porous-material oxidation can still produce heat and toxic products.

8.4 Fire spread mechanisms

Fire can spread through direct flame contact, convection, radiation, conduction, flying brands/embers, burning liquids, hot gases and structural or service pathways.

9. Fire extinguishment

Every extinguishing tactic should make sense when mapped back to the fire tetrahedron.

MechanismWhat changes?Examples
CoolingRemoves heat faster than it is generated.Water on suitable Class A fires.
Smothering / oxygen reductionReduces oxidiser concentration at the reaction zone.Foam blanket, inert gas in suitable systems.
Fuel removalStops or reduces fuel supply.Closing a gas valve, isolating a fuel line.
Chain-reaction interruptionInterferes with chemical reactions sustaining flame.Some dry chemical agents.

9.1 Water

Water is highly effective for many ordinary combustibles because of its high heat capacity and latent heat of vaporisation. It is not automatically appropriate for every fire. Electrical hazards, water-reactive materials and certain flammable-liquid scenarios require careful agent selection.

9.2 Foam

Firefighting foams can form a blanket over suitable liquid fuels, suppress vapour release and provide cooling. Foam selection must match fuel type and application.

9.3 Carbon dioxide

CO₂ can displace oxygen locally and provides little residue, making it useful for certain enclosed electrical and flammable-liquid applications. It has limited cooling and can be ineffective against deep-seated Class A fires.

9.4 Dry chemical

Many dry chemical agents act primarily through flame chemistry interruption and can be effective for rapidly developing flame fires. They may provide limited cooling and may not prevent re-ignition where hot fuel remains.

10. Fire scenarios & engineering thinking

Scenario A — Waste-paper basket fire

Likely fuel is Class A. Initial fire size may be small. Cooling is generally effective. The key engineering question is why ignition occurred and whether combustible accumulation, ignition sources or housekeeping controls are inadequate.

Scenario B — Petrol spill ignites

Think vapour, surface area, ignition source, spread and re-ignition. Water application can be inappropriate if it spreads the liquid. Appropriate foam or dry chemical tactics depend on the specific situation, equipment and training.

Scenario C — LPG leak ignites

The fire itself may be only one part of the hazard. Fuel isolation is critical. A leaking gas cylinder or vessel can continue feeding the flame and may create severe thermal exposure to the container.

Scenario D — Compartment fire with closed door

Consider ventilation, hot gas accumulation, smoke movement and the possibility of rapid changes when an opening is made. Door opening is not a neutral action; it can change the fire's oxygen supply.

Scenario E — BLEVE concept

A BLEVE is a boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion associated with a vessel containing a pressurised liquid exposed to conditions that cause vessel failure and rapid depressurisation. It can produce blast, projectiles and a fireball where combustible material is involved. Prevention depends strongly on vessel integrity, pressure control, separation and protection against severe external heating.

11. Practical & calculation exercises

Example calculation — heat energy

For introductory study, the sensible heat required to change temperature can be represented as:

Q = m × c × ΔT

where Q is heat energy, m is mass, c is specific heat capacity and ΔT is temperature change. In real fire engineering, phase changes, heat losses and changing material properties may need to be included.

Essential glossary

Auto-ignition temperature

Temperature at which ignition can occur without an external ignition source under specified conditions.

Backdraft

Rapid combustion associated with sudden oxygen introduction into a hot, oxygen-depleted, fuel-rich enclosure.

BLEVE

Boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion following failure of a vessel containing a pressurised liquid.

Combustion

Exothermic chemical reaction involving fuel and an oxidising species.

Flash point

Lowest temperature at which sufficient vapour is produced to form an ignitable mixture under specified test conditions.

Flashover

Rapid transition to widespread involvement of combustible surfaces in a compartment.

Heat release rate

Rate at which a fire releases energy, commonly expressed in kW or MW.

LEL

Lower explosive/flammable limit of a gas or vapour in air.

Pyrolysis

Thermal decomposition of material producing gases/vapours that may burn.
