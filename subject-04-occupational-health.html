1. Industrial Hygiene

Industrial hygiene is the science and practice of anticipating, recognising, evaluating and controlling workplace conditions that may cause injury or occupational illness.

Anticipate → Recognise → Evaluate → Control

Hazard vs exposure

A hazardous substance or condition does not automatically mean a worker has a harmful exposure. Risk depends on what is present, how much reaches the worker, by which route, for how long and under what conditions.

Hazard

Intrinsic potential to cause harm.

Exposure

Contact between worker and hazard.

Risk

Likelihood and severity of harm under the circumstances.

Exposure routes

RouteMeaningExample
InhalationBreathing contaminants into the respiratory system.Welding fumes, vapour, dust.
DermalSkin contact or absorption.Solvents, corrosives.
IngestionSwallowing contamination.Eating with contaminated hands.
InjectionEntry through puncture or high-pressure penetration.Sharps or hydraulic injection.

2. Exposure Assessment

Ask four questions: what agent? who is exposed? how much? and for how long?

Personal sampling

Sampler is positioned in the worker's breathing zone to estimate individual exposure.

Area sampling

Characterises conditions at a location but may not represent an individual's exposure.

Exposure limits

Occupational exposure limits are reference values established by competent authorities, standards organisations or company policy. Always verify the applicable jurisdiction, substance and averaging period.

There is no universal exposure limit for every substance. Different jurisdictions and standards may use different values and definitions.

3. Occupational Noise

Excessive noise can cause hearing loss and interfere with communication, warning signals and concentration.

Sound level

Sound pressure level is commonly expressed in decibels (dB). Occupational assessments often use A-weighting, expressed as dB(A), to approximate human hearing sensitivity.

Control hierarchy

Engineering

Enclosure, isolation, damping, quieter equipment and maintenance.

Administrative

Exposure management, scheduling and controlled access.

Hearing protection

Earplugs or earmuffs selected and fitted as part of a programme.

Key point: “Wear earplugs” is not a complete noise-control strategy. First ask whether noise can be reduced at source.

4. Heat Stress

Heat stress occurs when the body's ability to maintain thermal balance is overwhelmed. Risk depends on environmental conditions, workload, clothing and individual factors.

Air temperature

Higher temperatures reduce the body's ability to lose heat.

Radiant heat

Furnaces, hot surfaces and sunlight add thermal load.

Humidity

High humidity can reduce evaporative cooling.

Work rate

Muscular activity generates metabolic heat.

Prevention

  • Reduce radiant heat or shield workers.
  • Improve ventilation where appropriate.
  • Use suitable work-rest arrangements.
  • Provide appropriate hydration practices.
  • Use acclimatisation for workers entering hot environments.
  • Select clothing and PPE appropriate to the thermal environment.
Heat stroke is a medical emergency. Follow the workplace emergency procedure and obtain urgent medical assistance.

5. Illumination

Inadequate or excessive lighting can contribute to visual fatigue, errors, trips and poor task performance. Illumination is commonly measured in lux.

ProblemPossible effectControl
Too little lightDifficulty seeing hazards and task details.Improve level and distribution.
GlareDiscomfort and reduced visibility.Shield/reposition sources or modify surfaces.
FlickerDiscomfort or visual disturbance in susceptible situations.Correct lighting/electrical issues.
Uneven lightingAdaptation problems and shadows.Improve uniformity and task lighting.

6. Vibration

Hand-arm vibration

Associated with vibrating hand tools and can affect nerves, blood vessels, muscles and joints.

Whole-body vibration

Associated with vehicles and machinery and may contribute to musculoskeletal problems.

Controls

  1. Select lower-vibration equipment.
  2. Maintain tools and machinery.
  3. Improve work methods.
  4. Manage exposure time where necessary.
  5. Provide training and health surveillance where indicated.

7. Chemical Hazards

Chemical hazards include toxicity, corrosivity, flammability, reactivity, sensitisation, carcinogenicity and other health effects.

Safety Data Sheets

An SDS can provide information on identification, hazards, composition, first aid, firefighting, accidental release, handling/storage, exposure controls, PPE, physical properties, stability/reactivity and toxicology. Use the current SDS for the actual product.

Control hierarchy

Eliminate

Remove the hazardous chemical or process.

Substitute

Use a less hazardous material or process.

Engineering

Enclosure, isolation or local exhaust ventilation.

Administrative

Procedures, training and exposure-management practices.

PPE

Gloves, goggles, respirators and protective clothing where required.

Emergency controls

Spill response, eyewash, emergency shower and emergency planning.

Glove selection matters. Chemical resistance depends on the chemical, concentration, temperature, contact time and glove material.

8. Radiation

Radiation hazards can broadly be divided into ionising and non-ionising radiation.

TypeExamplesPotential concern
IonisingX-rays, gamma rays and certain radioactive emissions.Ionisation and biological tissue damage.
UVSunlight, arc welding.Skin and eye injury.
InfraredFurnaces, hot surfaces.Thermal exposure and eye/skin effects.
RF/microwaveSpecific industrial/communications equipment.Exposure depends on frequency, power and conditions.

Ionising-radiation protection

Time ↓    Distance ↑    Shielding ↑

Minimise time near the source, maximise distance where practical and use appropriate shielding. Follow competent radiation-protection requirements.

9. Ergonomics & Manual Handling

Ergonomics designs work to fit human capabilities and limitations. Musculoskeletal risk can result from combinations of force, posture, repetition, duration, vibration and inadequate recovery.

Assessment questions

  • What is the load weight and shape?
  • How far must it be moved?
  • Can mechanical assistance be used?
  • How frequent is the task?
  • Is the worker twisting, reaching or lifting from an awkward height?
  • Is the route clear and stable?
Best intervention: Change the task or equipment so excessive human force is no longer required. Lifting training should not be the only control.

10. Biological Hazards

Biological hazards include bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and biological materials capable of causing infection, allergy or other effects.

Healthcare

Infectious agents, sharps and contaminated materials.

Wastewater

Microorganisms and contaminated aerosols.

Laboratories

Organisms and biological materials under controlled conditions.

Controls

Controls can include hygiene, vaccination where appropriate, engineering containment, safe sharps systems, disinfection, waste management, appropriate PPE and exposure-response procedures.

11. Personal Protective Equipment

PPE is generally the last line of defence in the hierarchy of controls, although it remains essential when higher-level controls cannot fully eliminate exposure.

HazardPossible PPESelection principle
Eye/faceSafety glasses, goggles, face shieldMatch impact, splash, radiation or chemical hazard.
RespiratoryRespiratorCorrect contaminant, concentration, filter/cartridge and fit.
HandsProtective glovesMaterial compatibility and task-specific protection.
NoiseEarplugs/earmuffsSuitable attenuation, fit and compatibility.
BodyProtective clothingThermal, chemical, cut, arc or other hazard-specific performance.

Respiratory protection

Respiratory protection requires a programme, not merely a mask: hazard identification, correct selection, fit testing where applicable, medical considerations, maintenance, replacement and training.

Essential Glossary

Acute effect

Health effect occurring over a relatively short period after exposure.

Chronic effect

Health effect developing or persisting over a longer period.

Industrial hygiene

Anticipation, recognition, evaluation and control of workplace health hazards.

Occupational exposure limit

Reference value for workplace exposure under specified conditions.

PPE

Personal protective equipment used to reduce exposure.

Sampling

Collection or measurement used to characterise workplace exposure.

Surveillance

Systematic health or exposure monitoring intended to identify trends or effects.

Hierarchy of controls

Elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative controls and PPE.

SDS

Safety Data Sheet containing information about a hazardous product.
