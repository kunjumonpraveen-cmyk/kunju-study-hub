Subject 01 • From zero to professional practice

Fundamentals of Industrial Safety

This is not a chapter summary. It is a structured learning path designed to take a beginner from the basic meaning of safety to the point where they can identify hazards, assess risk, select controls, conduct inspections, investigate incidents and communicate safety requirements professionally.

What you should be able to do

By the end of this subject, you should think like a safety practitioner rather than someone who has simply memorised definitions.

Recognise hazards

Identify energy sources, unsafe conditions, unsafe acts, exposure pathways and conditions that can produce injury, illness, fire, environmental harm or asset loss.

Assess risk

Estimate the significance of risk using likelihood, consequence and exposure, while recognising the limitations of simple risk matrices.

Select controls

Apply the hierarchy of controls and distinguish elimination/substitution/engineering controls from weaker administrative and PPE controls.

Inspect workplaces

Plan and conduct a meaningful safety inspection, record evidence, identify root issues and assign corrective actions.

Communicate

Deliver toolbox talks, write clear observations, explain controls to workers and escalate serious hazards without ambiguity.

Investigate

Separate immediate causes from underlying and organisational causes and develop corrective actions that address system weaknesses.

The safety mindset

Industrial safety is the systematic management of hazards and risk. It is not simply “being careful”, wearing PPE or stopping work whenever something looks dangerous.

Core principle: A good safety professional does not ask only “Who made the mistake?” They ask “What conditions, systems, decisions, controls and incentives allowed the event to become possible?”

Safety, hazard and risk are different

TermMeaningExample
HazardA source or situation with potential to cause harm.Stored LPG, rotating machinery, exposed live conductor.
RiskThe combination of likelihood and consequence of harm occurring.Likelihood of an LPG release igniting and the possible consequences.
ControlA measure intended to eliminate or reduce risk.Isolation, guarding, ventilation, procedure or PPE.
IncidentAn event that resulted in or could have resulted in harm/loss.A dropped load that misses a worker.
AccidentCommonly used for an unwanted event causing injury, damage or loss; terminology varies by system.Worker injured by falling material.
Near missAn event with no actual harm but credible potential for harm.A worker nearly struck by a reversing vehicle.

Three questions to ask at any workplace

  1. What can hurt someone? Identify the hazard and the exposed people.
  2. How could it happen? Understand the initiating event, pathway and escalation.
  3. What prevents it? Identify existing controls and test whether they are reliable in real conditions.

Unit I — Foundations of Industrial Safety

Build the vocabulary and mental models used by safety professionals.

1.1 Why industrial safety exists

Industrial activity converts energy and materials into useful products and services. The same energy that makes industry productive can injure people when it is uncontrolled. Mechanical motion, electricity, pressure, heat, chemicals, gravity, radiation, vehicles and stored energy are all potential sources of harm.

People

Prevent fatality, injury, occupational disease and unnecessary exposure.

Process

Prevent loss of containment, fires, explosions, uncontrolled releases and process upsets.

Assets & environment

Reduce property damage, production loss, pollution and business interruption.

1.2 Accident causation

Serious events rarely have one simple cause. A useful investigation moves backward from the event through immediate causes, contributing factors and underlying system weaknesses.

Swiss Cheese model — understand the idea

Organisational defences can be imagined as several layers. Each layer has weaknesses or “holes”. An accident occurs when holes temporarily align and a hazard passes through multiple failed or inadequate barriers.

Use this model to avoid blaming the last person in the chain. Ask why the barrier failed, why it was weak, and why the organisation allowed that weakness to persist.

Domino theory

Older accident models describe events as a sequence where one failure contributes to the next. Modern practice usually treats accidents as more complex, but the basic lesson remains useful: remove or strengthen critical links in the causal chain.

Human error — don't stop the investigation here

Human error can be a symptom rather than a root cause. Examine workload, training, interface design, supervision, procedure quality, fatigue, time pressure, equipment condition and organisational priorities before concluding that “operator error” explains the event.

1.3 Unsafe act vs unsafe condition

Unsafe actUnsafe conditionBetter investigation question
Bypassing a guardGuard design makes access difficultWhy was bypassing possible and why was the guard not effective?
Walking under a suspended loadPedestrian route crosses lifting areaWhy is the traffic interface designed this way?
Not wearing eye protectionWrong PPE issued / poor availabilityWas suitable PPE selected, supplied and enforced?

1.4 Safety culture and leadership

Safety culture is reflected in what an organisation actually rewards, tolerates, prioritises and learns from—not merely what its policy says. A strong culture encourages reporting, learns from weak signals and treats serious hazards consistently even when production pressure exists.

Professional test: If a procedure says one thing but the normal work practice says another, the gap is a safety finding worth investigating.

1.5 Incident and near-miss reporting

A good report is factual, timely and specific. Separate observation from interpretation.

Observation: "At 10:20, a worker was observed using an angle grinder without the side handle installed." Interpretation: "The task may have increased the probability of loss of control." Action: "Stop the task, restore the manufacturer-required handle, inspect the tool and verify pre-use inspection requirements."

Unit II — Hazard Identification

Learn to systematically find what can cause harm before it becomes an incident.

2.1 Hazard families

Physical

Noise, vibration, heat, cold, radiation, pressure, illumination and mechanical energy.

Chemical

Flammable, toxic, corrosive, oxidising, reactive, carcinogenic or otherwise hazardous substances.

Biological

Microorganisms, biological agents, contaminated materials and occupational infection hazards.

Ergonomic

Manual handling, awkward posture, repetition, force, workstation design and fatigue.

Electrical

Shock, arc flash, arc blast, thermal effects, static electricity and electrical ignition sources.

Fire & explosion

Fuel, oxidiser, ignition, confinement, pressure, dust clouds, vapour clouds and escalation pathways.

2.2 Energy-based hazard identification

One powerful approach is to ask what energy exists in the task and how that energy could be released unexpectedly.

EnergyExamplesPotential consequenceTypical controls
GravitySuspended load, elevated platformCrush / struck-byExclusion zone, lifting controls, guardrails
ElectricalLive conductor, capacitorShock / arcIsolation, verification, guarding
PressureCompressed gas, steamRupture / projectileDesign protection, isolation, inspection
MechanicalRotating shaft, stored springEntanglement / impactGuarding, interlock, isolation
ChemicalToxic vapour, corrosive liquidExposure / fireSubstitution, containment, ventilation
ThermalHot surface, molten materialBurn / ignitionInsulation, guarding, separation

2.3 Hazard identification techniques

Workplace inspection

Walk the workplace systematically. Observe conditions, behaviours, equipment, access, housekeeping, emergency arrangements and interactions between people and plant.

Job Safety Analysis / Job Hazard Analysis

Break a job into logical steps, identify hazards at each step and define controls. Avoid making steps so broad that hazards disappear inside vague wording.

HAZID

A structured multidisciplinary method for identifying hazards associated with a facility, process, project or activity, usually at an early stage.

What-if analysis

Ask structured “What if…?” questions: What if containment fails? What if power is lost? What if a valve is left open? What if a detector fails?

2.4 Exposure and people at risk

A hazard without exposure may represent little immediate risk. Identify who can be exposed, how, how often, and whether vulnerable or unfamiliar persons could be affected.

  • Employees and contractors
  • Visitors and members of the public
  • Maintenance and emergency personnel
  • Young or inexperienced workers
  • Workers performing non-routine tasks

Unit III — Risk Assessment

Turn hazard information into decisions about what must be controlled first.

3.1 Risk assessment workflow

1 — Prepare

Define scope, task, location, people, equipment and available information.

2 — Identify

Find hazards, initiating events, exposures and possible consequences.

3 — Evaluate

Estimate risk and compare it with organisational criteria.

4 — Control

Select controls using the hierarchy of controls.

5 — Implement

Assign owners, resources, deadlines and verification.

6 — Review

Reassess after changes, incidents, new information or periodic review.

3.2 Likelihood and consequence

A simple qualitative matrix often represents risk as:

Risk ≈ Likelihood × Consequence

This is a useful communication model, not a law of physics. Risk matrices can create false precision if their categories are poorly defined.

LikelihoodInterpretation
RareHighly unlikely under defined conditions, but not necessarily impossible.
UnlikelyCould occur but is not expected during normal operations.
PossibleCredible occurrence under foreseeable conditions.
LikelyExpected to occur repeatedly or under common conditions.
Almost certainFrequent or highly predictable occurrence.

3.3 Hierarchy of controls

LevelControlExampleGeneral reliability
1EliminationRemove the hazardous task entirely.Highest
2SubstitutionReplace a hazardous chemical with a safer alternative.High
3EngineeringGuarding, enclosure, ventilation, interlock.High
4AdministrativeProcedure, training, scheduling, signage.Moderate / variable
5PPEHelmet, gloves, eye protection, respirator.Last line
Common mistake: “PPE is provided” is not the same as “the hazard is adequately controlled.” PPE depends on correct selection, fit, availability, use, maintenance and human behaviour.

3.4 Residual risk

After controls are implemented, some risk may remain. This is residual risk. A professional assessment states what remains, who accepts it, and what monitoring or further controls are required.

3.5 Dynamic risk assessment

Risk assessment is not always a document completed once. Conditions can change: weather, equipment condition, simultaneous operations, personnel, process state, access and emergency conditions can all alter risk.

Unit IV — Safety Management

Move from individual hazards to systems that consistently control risk.

4.1 Safety management system

A management system links policy, planning, implementation, verification and improvement. A mature system does not depend on one enthusiastic safety officer.

Leadership

Policy, accountability, resources and visible commitment.

Planning

Hazard identification, objectives, legal requirements and risk controls.

Implementation

Competence, communication, procedures, operational controls and emergency preparedness.

Assurance

Inspection, audit, monitoring, incident learning and management review.

4.2 Safety inspection vs safety audit

InspectionAudit
Usually focuses on physical conditions and operational compliance.Examines whether a management system or defined requirements are systematically implemented.
Often frequent and field-oriented.Usually structured, evidence-based and broader.
Example: inspect scaffolding.Example: audit the scaffolding management system.

4.3 Toolbox talk

A toolbox talk should be short, task-specific and interactive. A strong structure is:

  1. Define today's task and location.
  2. Identify the main hazards.
  3. Explain critical controls.
  4. Discuss abnormal conditions and stop-work triggers.
  5. Confirm PPE and emergency arrangements.
  6. Ask workers to identify hazards you may have missed.

4.4 Permit-to-work concept

Permit-to-work systems control specified high-risk work by formally confirming prerequisites, isolations, precautions, responsibilities and authorisation. A permit is not itself a risk assessment and is not a substitute for competent planning.

4.5 Management of Change

Any change in equipment, material, process, software, operating envelope, staffing or procedure can introduce new hazards. MOC asks what is changing, why, what hazards arise, what safeguards are affected, whether drawings/procedures/training need updating, and who approves the change.

4.6 Safety performance indicators

Lagging indicators

Record outcomes after failure: injuries, fires, spills, property damage.

Leading indicators

Measure preventive activity or barrier health: critical inspection completion, overdue actions, safety observations, emergency drill performance.

Unit V — Practical Safety

Now convert knowledge into field competence.

5.1 Workplace inspection method

  1. Review previous incidents, risk assessments and open actions.
  2. Define the route and inspection scope.
  3. Observe work without immediately changing worker behaviour.
  4. Look at people, plant, process, environment and management controls.
  5. Record evidence: location, condition, exposure and possible consequence.
  6. Immediately address imminent serious danger according to site procedure.
  7. Assign corrective actions with owners and deadlines.
  8. Verify closure and effectiveness.

5.2 Writing a strong safety observation

Weak

“Housekeeping is poor.”

Strong

“At the north access route, approximately 6 m of walkway was obstructed by hoses and scrap material, reducing clear pedestrian access. Remove materials and establish a designated hose route.”

5.3 Corrective action quality

A weak action says “Tell workers to be careful.” A stronger action changes the system: redesign the access route, install a guard, eliminate the need to enter the hazard zone, revise the procedure, provide a suitable tool, or introduce verification.

5.4 Accident investigation

Protect life first, preserve evidence, control secondary hazards, identify witnesses and establish a factual timeline. Then analyse causal factors.

EVENT ↓ Immediate causes ↓ Contributing factors ↓ Underlying causes ↓ Organisational / system causes ↓ Corrective actions ↓ Effectiveness verification

5.5 Root cause analysis

“Why?” questions are useful but should not be used mechanically. Stop when you reach a controllable system weakness that, if corrected, would materially reduce the probability of recurrence.

Important: A root cause is not automatically the deepest sentence you can write. It is a causal factor that is actionable and meaningful for prevention.

Core professional tools

JSA / JHA

Task-level hazard analysis used before or during work planning.

Risk Assessment

Structured evaluation of hazard, exposure, likelihood, consequence and controls.

HAZID

Broad, multidisciplinary identification of hazards for facilities, projects or processes.

HAZOP

Structured examination using guidewords such as No, More, Less, Reverse and Other than to identify process deviations and consequences.

Bow-Tie

Maps causes on the left, top event in the centre, consequences on the right, with preventive and mitigative barriers.

Permit-to-Work

Formal control for defined high-risk work and required precautions.

Bow-Tie thinking

THREATS → PREVENTIVE BARRIERS → TOP EVENT → MITIGATIVE BARRIERS → CONSEQUENCES Example: Flammable release → isolation / containment / detection → loss of containment → gas detection / ESD / fire protection / emergency response → fire / explosion / exposure

Case studies — learn to think like an investigator

Case 1 — Worker slips on an oily floor

Initial finding: Worker slipped near a pump.

Weak conclusion: “Worker was careless.”

Better questions: Where did the oil come from? Was there a leak? Why was it not detected? Was the drainage adequate? Was the pump maintained? Was the area inspected? Was there a spill-response system? Were previous leaks reported?

Potential controls: eliminate leak source, improve containment, redesign drainage, preventive maintenance, inspection criteria and rapid spill response.

Case 2 — Forklift nearly strikes a pedestrian

Investigate route design, visibility, speed, pedestrian segregation, reversing alarms, mirrors, lighting, traffic rules, driver competence and delivery schedules. Do not assume training alone will solve a poorly designed traffic interface.

Case 3 — Worker receives an electric shock

Establish the electrical state, isolation status, test-before-touch process, equipment condition, competence, permit requirements and whether the circuit was correctly identified. Consider upstream organisational causes if isolation was routinely bypassed.

Case 4 — Repeated hand injuries from a machine

Repeated injuries are a strong signal that the existing control strategy is inadequate. Investigate guarding, interlocks, access requirements, production pressures, maintenance, machine design and whether workers are forced to reach into the danger zone.

Field exercises

Do these in a real or simulated workplace. Do not enter restricted or hazardous areas without authorisation and required controls.

Essential glossary

ALARP

As Low As Reasonably Practicable; risk reduction principle used in some regulatory and organisational frameworks.

Barrier

A measure that prevents an initiating event or limits its consequences.

Competence

Combination of knowledge, skills, experience and behaviours required to perform safely.

Exposure

Contact of a person with a hazard or hazardous agent.

Hazard

Source or situation with potential for harm.

Hierarchy of controls

Preferred order for reducing risk from elimination through PPE.

Incident

Unwanted event that caused or could have caused harm or loss.

JSA

Job Safety Analysis; breaks work into steps and identifies hazards and controls.

MOC

Management of Change; structured control of changes that may affect risk.

Near miss

Event with no actual harm but credible potential for harm.

Residual risk

Risk remaining after controls are applied.

Risk

Combination of likelihood and consequence associated with a hazard.
